\begin{abstract}
Gradient staleness from asynchronous training degrades convergence and
interacts non-trivially with momentum.
All experiments in this work \emph{simulate} asynchronous SGD in a single
sequential process by injecting stale gradients from a buffer; no real
multi-process or distributed training is performed.
We investigate three questions: (1)~how does delay affect convergence speed
and final accuracy; (2)~how does momentum interact with delay, does the
delay distribution type matter, and can theory-guided scheduling recover
lost performance; and (3)~whether gradient staleness acts as a regularizer
analogous to dropout.
% [Q1 TEAMMATE Ilia: one sentence summarising convergence findings]
For question~2, we show that $\beta{=}0.9$ collapses catastrophically under
deterministic delay (dropping from 90.3\% to below 11\% at $\tau{=}20$),
consistent with Liu et al.~\cite{liu2018momentum}.
Crucially, switching to \emph{geometric} delay (matching the M/M/1 model
of Mitliagkas et al.~\cite{mitliagkas2014async}) reverses this asymmetry:
$\beta{=}0$ becomes far more resilient (82\% vs.\ 52\% at $\mathrm{E}[\tau]{=}20$),
while high momentum collapses even faster due to rare extreme-lag events.
An adaptive schedule tracking the Liu bound recovers up to $+5.9$\,pp
over fixed-$\beta$ baselines.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =========================================================================

Stochastic gradient descent is the workhorse of deep learning, but
wall-clock training time on a single processor is a bottleneck at scale.
Asynchronous SGD~\cite{recht2011hogwild} sidesteps synchronization overhead
by allowing workers to apply gradients without waiting for each other,
producing \emph{stale} gradients: a gradient computed on $\theta_t$ is
applied at time $t{+}\tau$ when the model has already advanced to
$\theta_{t+\tau}$.
The practical impact of this staleness on accuracy and convergence depends
critically on both the magnitude and the \emph{distribution} of the delay.
Studying this empirically in a real distributed system introduces
confounders (hardware variance, network jitter, synchronization overhead)
that make it difficult to isolate the effect of staleness itself.
We therefore adopt a \emph{controlled simulation} approach: a single
sequential process injects gradients from a buffer, precisely controlling
the staleness at each step.
This allows clean isolation of delay effects but does not capture
system-level phenomena such as worker heterogeneity or communication overlap.

Three concrete questions motivate this project.
First, does delay degrade accuracy monotonically, and does it slow
convergence uniformly across training phases?
Second, how does explicit momentum interact with staleness, does the
distribution of delays (deterministic vs.\ stochastic) change this
interaction, and can theoretical guarantees guide adaptive schedule design?
Third, can gradient noise from staleness act as a regularizer analogous
to dropout, improving generalization?

% [Q1 TEAMMATE Ilia: expand on question 1 and your contributions here]
% [Q3 TEAMMATE Aleksandar: expand on question 3 and your contributions here]

Our contributions for question~2 are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A $\tau \times \beta$ grid (45 runs) showing that $\beta{=}0.9$
        is catastrophically unstable at any nonzero \emph{deterministic}
        delay, consistent with the Liu et al.\ bound
        $\tau \lesssim (1-\mu)^2/\eta$.
  \item A matched geometric-delay sweep ($\tau_t \sim \mathrm{Geom}(1/M)$,
        45 runs) revealing that the delay distribution type fundamentally
        changes the momentum-delay interaction: at the same average staleness,
        stochastic delay is safer for $\beta{=}0$ but more dangerous for
        $\beta{=}0.9$.
  \item A negative result for the Mitliagkas equivalence
        conjecture~\cite{mitliagkas2014async} even under geometric delay,
        confirming it does not hold empirically on ResNet-20/CIFAR-10.
  \item Negative explicit momentum ($\beta \in [-0.9, 0]$) as a
        theoretically grounded compensation, yielding a $+13.3$\,pp gain
        at $\tau{=}20$.
  \item An adaptive momentum schedule tracking
        $\mu_\mathrm{safe}(\eta_t) = 1 - \sqrt{\tau \eta_t}$ as the LR
        decays, consistently outperforming all fixed-$\beta$ baselines under
        both delay regimes.
\end{itemize}

\section{Models and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
% =========================================================================

\subsection{Common Setup}

All experiments train ResNet-20~\cite{he2016resnet} on
CIFAR-10~\cite{krizhevsky2009cifar} (50k/10k split).
Base learning rate $\eta{=}0.1$, decayed by $\times 0.1$ at epochs 30 and
40 (50 total), batch size 128, weight decay $10^{-4}$.
Every configuration is run with three random seeds \{42, 123, 456\}; we
report mean $\pm$ standard deviation of final test accuracy.

\subsection{Delay Simulation}

All delay experiments run in a \textbf{single sequential process}.
No workers, threads, or network communication are involved.
Staleness is injected artificially via a gradient buffer: at each step,
a fresh gradient is computed and stored, and a \emph{stale} gradient
from the buffer is applied to the parameters.
This design gives exact, reproducible control over the delay distribution
and eliminates confounders from real distributed systems, at the cost of
not modelling system-level effects.

\paragraph{Deterministic (FIFO) delay.}
A FIFO buffer of depth $\tau$ is maintained.
After computing a fresh gradient on $\theta_t$, we push it onto the buffer
and apply the oldest buffered gradient to the parameters.
This gives a \emph{deterministic} staleness of exactly $\tau$ steps at
every update --- a controlled baseline that isolates delay magnitude.

\paragraph{Geometric (stochastic) delay.}
To match the M/M/1 queue model from which Mitliagkas et
al.~\cite{mitliagkas2014async} derive their implicit-momentum result,
we sample a fresh delay at every step:
\begin{equation}
  \tau_t \sim \mathrm{Geom}(1/M) - 1, \qquad \mathrm{E}[\tau_t] = M-1.
\end{equation}
We choose $M \in \{1, 3, 6, 11, 21\}$ so that $\mathrm{E}[\tau] \in
\{0, 2, 5, 10, 20\}$ matches the deterministic grid exactly, enabling
direct comparison at the same average staleness.
A rolling buffer stores the last $5M$ gradient snapshots; each step
looks up the one sampled by $\tau_t$.

\paragraph{Optimizer.}
PyTorch's \texttt{SGD} rejects $\beta < 0$, so we implement a custom
\texttt{SGDMomentum} supporting any $\beta \in \mathbb{R}$:
\begin{equation}
  v_t = \beta v_{t-1} + g_t,\qquad \theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \eta v_t.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Theoretical grounding.}
Liu et al.~\cite{liu2018momentum} prove that for Async-MSGD to converge
on non-convex objectives the delay must satisfy $\tau \lesssim (1-\mu)^2/\eta$.
Inverting this gives a \emph{safe momentum ceiling}:
\begin{equation}
  \mu_\mathrm{safe}(\eta,\tau) = 1 - \sqrt{\tau\cdot\eta}.
  \label{eq:liu}
\end{equation}
At $\eta{=}0.1$: $\mu_\mathrm{safe} = 0.29$ ($\tau{=}5$), $0.0$
($\tau{=}10$), $-0.41$ ($\tau{=}20$).
This bound was derived for deterministic fixed delay; applying it to
geometric delay via $\mathrm{E}[\tau] = M-1$ is an approximation
we test empirically.

\paragraph{Experiments.}
Table~\ref{tab:exp-summary} summarises all experiment groups for
question~2.
Exp~F sweeps negative explicit momentum $\beta \in \{-0.1,\ldots,-0.9\}$.
Exp~G evaluates four adaptive schedules: \texttt{at\_bound}
($\mu = \mu_\mathrm{safe}(\eta_t,\tau)$), \texttt{below\_bound}
($\mu_\mathrm{safe}-0.3$), \texttt{above\_bound}
($\min(0.95,\,\mu_\mathrm{safe}+0.3)$), and \texttt{neg\_ramp}
(negative Mitliagkas phase, then $\mu_\mathrm{safe}$ after first LR decay).
Geo~Exp~A tests the Mitliagkas equivalence under stochastic delay.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Deterministic Delay: Momentum Collapse and Recovery}

Figure~\ref{fig:momentum-collapse} shows final test accuracy across the
$\tau \times \beta$ grid.
Without delay ($\tau{=}0$), momentum helps: $\beta{=}0.9$ achieves 90.3\%
vs.\ 87.2\% for $\beta{=}0$.
Under delay, this reverses sharply.
At $\tau{=}5$, $\beta{=}0.9$ drops to 70.5\% while $\beta{=}0$ retains
85.3\% ($-14.8$\,pp gap).
At $\tau{=}10$, $\beta{=}0.9$ collapses to 28.7\% while $\beta{=}0$
holds at 81.6\%.
At $\tau{=}20$, $\beta{=}0.9$ reaches 10.6\% (random-class level).
These results are fully consistent with Eq.~\eqref{eq:liu}: at $\eta{=}0.1$,
$\beta{=}0.9$ is unsafe for any $\tau \geq 1$.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{plot_C_momentum_benefit}
  \caption{Final test accuracy vs.\ delay $\tau$ for three fixed momentum
           values (deterministic delay). $\beta{=}0.9$ is best at $\tau{=}0$
           but collapses under any delay; $\beta{=}0$ is most robust.
           Mean $\pm$ std, 3 seeds.}
  \label{fig:momentum-collapse}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Negative momentum (Exp~F).}
At $\tau{=}20$, the best fixed negative value ($\beta{=}{-}0.9$) reaches
71.0\%, a $+13.3$\,pp gain over the $\beta{=}0$ baseline of 57.7\%.
Gains diminish at smaller delays: at $\tau{=}5$, large negative $\beta$
underperforms $\beta{=}0$ (Appendix, Fig.~\ref{fig:neg-momentum}).

\paragraph{Adaptive schedule (Exp~G).}
Figure~\ref{fig:adaptive-summary} compares four adaptive schedules against
fixed baselines, with a dashed line marking the no-delay ceiling (90.3\%).
The \texttt{above\_bound} schedule ($\mu = \min(0.95,\,\mu_\mathrm{safe}+0.3)$)
consistently achieves the highest accuracy: 88.3\% at $\tau{=}5$
($-2.0$\,pp below ceiling), 85.1\% at $\tau{=}10$, and 63.6\% at
$\tau{=}20$ --- outperforming $\beta{=}0$ by $+3.0$, $+3.5$, and
$+5.9$\,pp respectively.
Operating \emph{above} the Liu bound by a moderate offset does not cause
divergence, suggesting the bound is conservative for practical LR schedules
with decay.
Exp~H (Appendix, Fig.~\ref{fig:above-bound-sweep}) sweeps the offset
$\delta \in \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7\}$ and confirms $\delta{=}0.3$ as the
sweet spot: larger offsets degrade at $\tau{=}20$ while smaller ones
leave accuracy on the table.
The \texttt{neg\_ramp} schedule consistently underperforms, likely because
the negative phase corrupts the momentum buffer before the switch.

\subsection{Delay Distribution Matters: Geometric vs.\ Deterministic}

Figure~\ref{fig:geo-comparison} compares deterministic and geometric delay
at matched average staleness $\mathrm{E}[\tau] = \tau$.
The two regimes diverge qualitatively at high staleness.

\paragraph{$\beta{=}0$ (no momentum).}
Geometric delay is substantially more resilient.
At $\mathrm{E}[\tau]{=}20$ (M=21), geo reaches 82.1\% vs.\ det 52.3\%
($+29.8$\,pp).
The reason: under geometric delay, roughly $1/M$ steps receive a
completely fresh gradient ($\tau_t{=}0$), which periodically corrects
the parameter trajectory even at high average staleness.
Deterministic delay offers no such recovery --- every gradient is exactly
$\tau$ steps stale.

\paragraph{$\beta{=}0.9$ (high momentum).}
The roles reverse at medium-to-high staleness.
At $\mathrm{E}[\tau]{=}10$, geometric delay collapses completely
(9.9\%, random guessing) while deterministic delay degrades more
gracefully (28.7\%).
The geometric tail produces occasional extreme lags ($\tau_t \gg
\mathrm{E}[\tau]$) that corrupt the momentum accumulator; with $\beta{=}0.9$
the velocity is sticky and cannot recover.
Both regimes collapse at $\mathrm{E}[\tau]{=}20$.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{plot_geo_step3_comparison}
  \caption{Final test accuracy vs.\ average staleness for deterministic
           (solid) and geometric (dashed) delay.
           For $\beta{=}0$ and $\beta{=}0.5$, geometric delay is more
           resilient at high staleness.
           For $\beta{=}0.9$, geometric delay collapses faster due to
           momentum amplifying rare extreme-lag events.}
  \label{fig:geo-comparison}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Mitliagkas equivalence under geometric delay (Exp~A geo).}
Mitliagkas et al.\ conjecture that $M$ async workers with geometric
staleness induce implicit momentum $\mu_S = (M{-}1)/M$, making
$(M,\,\beta{=}0)$ equivalent to $(\tau{=}0,\,\beta{=}\mu_S)$.
Even under geometric delay --- the exact distributional setting of the
proof --- the equivalence does not hold empirically.
At $M{=}3$ ($\mu_S{=}0.667$), the geometric-delay run reaches 85.8\%
vs.\ 89.2\% for the explicit-momentum baseline ($-3.4$\,pp gap).
At $M{=}6$ ($\mu_S{=}0.833$), the gap widens to $-4.5$\,pp
(see Appendix, Fig.~\ref{fig:geo-equivalence}).
The stochastic gradient variance introduced by geometric delay is not
captured by the explicit momentum analogue.

\paragraph{Adaptive schedule under geometric delay (Exp~G geo).}
The Liu-bound schedule transfers to geometric delay: \texttt{above\_bound}
still outperforms fixed $\beta{=}0$ at all $M$ values tested (Appendix,
Fig.~\ref{fig:geo-adaptive}).
However, gains are smaller than under deterministic delay, and variance
across seeds is higher, consistent with the increased gradient noise
from stochastic staleness.

\section{Discussion}
% =========================================================================

Our results support a clear picture: momentum and gradient staleness
compound each other's harm, but the nature of this interaction depends
on the \emph{distribution} of delays, not just their average magnitude.

Under deterministic delay, the Liu et al.\ bound~\cite{liu2018momentum}
correctly predicts the instability regime and guides the adaptive schedule
design.
The finding that operating $\delta{=}0.3$ above the bound is empirically
safe suggests the bound is conservative, likely because it must hold for
arbitrary non-convex landscapes whereas CIFAR-10/ResNet-20 is comparatively
well-behaved after LR decay.

Under geometric delay, the picture is more nuanced.
The stochastic variance introduced by the geometric tail helps $\beta{=}0$
training (periodic fresh gradients act as a corrective signal) but
catastrophically amplifies the harm for $\beta{=}0.9$ (the heavy tail
corrupts the momentum accumulator irreversibly at intermediate delays).
This suggests that practitioners choosing momentum for asynchronous systems
should account for the staleness distribution, not just its mean: geometric
or heavy-tailed delays call for more conservative momentum coefficients
than deterministic analyses would predict.

The Mitliagkas equivalence conjecture is not reproduced even under the
geometric delay distribution it was derived for.
We attribute this to gradient variance: the equivalence is a spectral
argument about the update operator, but in finite-epoch training on
ResNet-20, the additional noise from stochastic delay prevents the model
from attaining the same accuracy as explicit momentum at $\tau{=}0$.

\paragraph{Simulation scope and limitations.}
All findings are based on single-process gradient-buffer simulation.
Real async SGD introduces additional phenomena not modelled here: variable
worker speed, communication latency, memory consistency across processes,
and batch-norm statistics that diverge per worker.
The geometric delay model is theoretically motivated (M/M/1 queue), but
real staleness distributions depend on hardware and workload in ways that
may deviate from geometric.
Our results should therefore be interpreted as isolating the effect of
\emph{delay magnitude and distribution on the optimization dynamics}, not
as direct performance predictions for a production distributed system.

\section{Summary}
% =========================================================================

The delay distribution type is as important as delay magnitude for
momentum-based async SGD.
Geometric delay is more forgiving for zero-momentum training but more
dangerous for high momentum; an adaptive schedule calibrated to the Liu
et al.\ bound recovers most lost accuracy under both regimes, with the
empirically optimal point lying slightly above the conservative theoretical
ceiling.
